Weighted Pull-Ups: When to Start and How to Progress

October 2, 2026 · 6 min read

There comes a point in every pull-up journey when adding reps stops feeling like progress. Sets of 15 take forever, the last few reps get sloppy, and the workout turns into an endurance test. That's usually the sign it's time for the next step: hanging a little weight from your waist.

Weighted pull-ups are the simplest way to keep getting stronger once bodyweight gets easy. This guide covers when you're ready, what gear to use, how to program them, and how to track the work so you keep seeing progress.

Why add weight at all?

Strength and endurance live in different rep ranges. Sets of 3 to 8 hard reps build maximal strength; sets of 15 and up mostly build the ability to keep going. Once your bodyweight set climbs past a dozen or so, every extra rep teaches your body more about endurance and less about pulling hard.

Adding weight puts you back in the strength range without changing the exercise. The payoff shows up everywhere: stronger lats and arms, a tougher grip, and, often surprisingly, more bodyweight reps. Many people who spend a few months on weighted work come back to an unweighted max test and beat their old record without having trained for it directly.

Are you ready?

A common guideline is to start adding weight once you can do around 8 to 10 clean, full-range reps. More important than the exact number is the quality of those reps:

  • Full range — from a dead hang with straight arms to your chin clearly over the bar
  • No kipping — no swinging, kicking, or using momentum to get up
  • Controlled descent — you lower yourself rather than dropping
  • Pain-free — no nagging elbow or shoulder pain during or after training

If your reps get shorter or swingier as the set goes on, build more bodyweight volume first. Adding weight to a shaky rep just makes the shaky part heavier.

How to add the weight

  • Dip belt. A belt with a chain that holds weight plates or a kettlebell between your legs. It keeps the load low and close to your center of mass, scales to any weight, and is the standard choice once you get serious.
  • Weight vest. Comfortable and quick to put on, and it spreads the load across your torso. Great for light-to-moderate weights and for doing several exercises in one session, though most vests top out well below what a belt can hold.
  • Dumbbell between your feet. No extra gear needed: grip a light dumbbell between your feet or ankles. It works for small loads, but it's awkward to set up and limits how much you can safely hold.
  • Backpack. A backpack with books or water bottles is a fine way to try weighted pull-ups at home. Pack it tightly so nothing shifts, and wear both straps snugly.

Whatever you use, get into position from a box or step rather than jumping up with the load, and lower yourself to the ground under control at the end of a set.

Your first weighted sets

Start light, lighter than your ego wants. For most people, 2.5 to 5 kg (5 to 10 lb) is plenty. The right starting load is one you can lift for 5 or 6 clean reps with a couple of reps still in the tank.

The first few sessions are mostly about getting used to the feel: the extra pull on your grip, keeping the weight from swinging, and holding your form under load. Resist the urge to find your max. That comes later, and it'll be higher if you build up to it.

A simple way to progress

The easiest progression method for weighted pull-ups is double progression: first add reps, then add weight.

  1. Pick a rep range, such as 3 sets of 5 to 8 reps.
  2. Start with a weight that lets you do 5 clean reps on every set.
  3. Each session, try to add a rep to one or more sets.
  4. When you hit 8 reps on all three sets, add a small amount of weight (1.25 to 2.5 kg, or 2.5 to 5 lb) and drop back to 5 reps.

Small jumps matter. Pulling strength builds slowly, and a 5 kg jump can feel like a wall when 2.5 kg would have felt like the next step. If your gym doesn't have small plates, a pair of fractional plates or even a full water bottle in a backpack will do.

Two weighted sessions a week is enough for most people, with 2 to 3 minutes of rest between heavy sets. Keep some bodyweight volume in your week too: easy sets on other days keep your rep endurance and give you extra practice at the movement without much added fatigue.

Technique under load

A weighted rep should look exactly like a good bodyweight rep, only harder. Things that tend to slip when the weight goes on:

  • Range of motion. Half reps creep in first. Start every rep from straight arms and finish with your chin over the bar.
  • Swinging. A hanging weight wants to sway. Brace your core and squeeze your legs together so the load stays still.
  • Shoulder position. Start each rep by pulling your shoulders down and back, away from your ears, before you bend your arms.
  • The descent. Lower under control. Dropping into the bottom with extra weight is hard on your elbows and shoulders.

What counts as strong?

Benchmarks vary a lot with training history, body size, and how strict the reps are, so treat these as loose reference points rather than targets:

  • Adding around 25% of your body weight for a set of 5 is a solid intermediate level.
  • Adding around 50% of your body weight for a single rep is an advanced milestone that takes most people years of training.

It also helps to think in terms of total load: your body weight plus the added weight. If you lose 3 kg of body weight and add 3 kg to the belt, you're lifting the same total, and your numbers are holding steady rather than going backwards.

Track weighted sets separately

Weighted training only works if you know what you lifted last time, and that means recording the weight along with the reps. Two things make the log much more useful:

  • Compare like with like. A set of 6 with 10 kg and a set of 6 at bodyweight are different achievements. Mixing them into one “best set” hides your progress in both.
  • Keep separate records. Your heaviest weight and your most reps with added weight are the numbers that tell you whether your weighted training is working.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Adding weight too fast. If your reps drop off sharply after a weight jump, the jump was too big. Go back down and use smaller steps.
  • Going to failure on every set. Grinding out the last rep with extra weight builds fatigue faster than strength. Finish most sets with a rep or two left.
  • Ignoring your elbows. Elbow pain is the most common complaint with heavy pulling. If it shows up, reduce the weight or the number of weighted sessions, and get persistent pain checked by a professional.
  • Dropping bodyweight work entirely. Weighted sets build strength, bodyweight sets keep your endurance and movement practice. You want both.

The bottom line

Once you can do 8 to 10 strict pull-ups, adding weight is the most direct way to keep getting stronger. Start light, progress in small steps, keep your reps as clean as your bodyweight ones, and record the weight with every set. A few months from now, the number on your belt will tell you exactly how far you've come.
